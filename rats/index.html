01 — Non-negotiable

Big safety things

Four common pet-store products cause most preventable injuries in pet rats. Rule these out before you shop for anything else.

Avoid

Bare wire wheels

  • No crossbar — rats easily catch their tails and are severely injured.
  • Running surface must be plastic, or plastic-coated.
  • At least 16″ in diameter. Yes, this is big — big enough for your largest rat to run with their back flat.
  • Good wheels are available from exotic pet stores, or even Etsy.
Avoid

Exposed wire floors

Rats are susceptible to a condition called bumblefoot. Too much walking on wire surfaces causes sores and calluses that deform the feet and are prone to infection. Eliminate wire flooring, or limit it severely.

Avoid

Wood shavings and corncob bedding

  • Wood shavings don't absorb urine and can contain substances harmful to rats. Aspen shavings are the exception — only from a reputable brand such as Carefresh or Kaytee.
  • Corncob is prone to growing mold and fungi.
  • Look for bedding labeled “dust-free,” “dust-removed,” or similar. Rats have extremely sensitive respiratory systems and react to small particulates and strong fragrances.
Why research facilities use corncob anyway: it can be autoclaved or irradiated, which kills spores and pathogens. That isn't possible at home.
Do this

Corner litter boxes

Most rats can be litter trained, so corner litter boxes are a good idea. Double-check the dimensions if ordering online — product images can be deceptive.

02 — Housing

A well-ventilated cage, with shelves and ramps

Aquariums are terrible for rats, and most rodents, because the air can't circulate.

What to look for

  • Powder-coated metal, with at least two shelves.
  • Bars no more than about ½″ apart.
  • Bigger is better, within reason — but each rat should have at least 1.5–2 ft³.
  • A drop-in design or plastic pull-out tray is ideal, since it reduces the amount of wire surface.
  • Second-hand cages can be a great deal — just disinfect thoroughly before use.

Soft furnishings

  • Cover most horizontal surfaces with smooth fabric liners, like fleece.
  • Avoid loopy fabrics such as towels — toenails get caught in the loops.
  • There are a ton of great Etsy shops selling fleece hammocks, shelf covers, and beds in any shape and size.
  • Wash any new bedding before using it.
  • Items marketed for ferrets, or even birds, are often great for rats too.
Critter Nation makes excellent cages. The single story is adequate for 2 large or 3 small rats, with hanging beds and hammocks. The double is better, but more expensive — see the range at Midwest Homes for Pets.

03 — Health

What to watch for, and who to call

Every rat carries mycoplasma

All rats carry a bacterium called mycoplasma — “myco” for short. Most strains of lab rats do not, but are likely to acquire it after leaving the controlled research facility.

  • For well-bred rats it may never be a problem. In unhealthy conditions it presents itself, usually as a respiratory infection.
  • Any change in respiration — labored breathing, rattling, crackling, clicking, excessive sneezing — should be looked at by a vet.
  • Adopting from a responsible breeder (or a lab) is the best, though not guaranteed, way to avoid it.
  • It is highly communicable. Don't interact with rats at pet stores, no ratty play-dates with friends' rats, and wash your hands after handling any other rodent even if it seems perfectly healthy.

Rat Guide has an excellent article on mycoplasmosis.

Porphyrin

Rats secrete a reddish substance called porphyrin from their eyes and nose. Occasional discharge is normal. Excessive build-up can signal illness or stress — particularly when it's dried and crusty.

Rats are very good at hiding illness

Signs worth acting on:

  • Hunched posture
  • Sudden changes in weight
  • Lumps, bumps, scabs, hair loss
  • Squinting, pinned ears, other changes in facial expression
  • Hind leg weakness

Hind leg degeneration

  • Rats, particularly males, can be prone to hind leg degeneration (HLD) as they age.
  • A sudden loss of function is likely due to trauma and should be treated immediately.
  • Gradual onset is not generally painful, and many rats adapt just fine. Discuss with your vet how to keep them comfortable and how to assess quality of life.
Find the vet before you get the rats. Most regular clinics won't see rats, and even those that do often aren't versed in the most up-to-date treatments. Find a good exotic vet in your area early — they book out far in advance, and even the best-kept animals will need a visit for something in their lifetime.

04 — Behavior

Bruxing, boggling, and biting

  • Rats have a polyphasic sleep cycle — multiple periods of sleep and wakefulness — but are most active at night.
  • When happy, rats grind their teeth. This is called bruxing. It can occasionally signal stress, but is usually positive.
  • Because of the anatomy of their skulls, bruxing sometimes makes their eyes bulge in and out — boggling. It looks alarming and is perfectly normal, a sign of contentment.
  • Rats rarely bite, and almost never out of aggression. Set them up for success: wash your hands after touching food, and keep your fingers out of the way when offering treats.

05 — Before you adopt

Choosing a sex

Every rat is different, but some tendencies hold.

  • Males tend to be calmer and more affectionate toward humans; females tend to be more playful.
  • Unneutered males are not typically more aggressive toward each other — unlike mice — as long as there are no females around.
  • Males develop a musky smell and are more prone to scent marking than females.
  • They can also develop a yellowish, greasy build-up on their backs called buck grease. It doesn't smell and is harmless.

Neutering and spaying

  • Neutering rats carries minimal risk with the proper vet.
  • Preemptively spaying females is less common. It reduces the risk of mammary tumors and other hormone-related problems, but is higher-risk and more invasive than neutering.
  • There is also an implant that chemically sterilizes rats — less invasive and less expensive, but not permanent.
  • Discuss the options with your vet.
A note on tumors: while not nearly as at-risk as females, male rats are more prone to mammary tumors than males of other species — mammary tissue on rodents is extensive. Obesity significantly increases the risk.

06 — Socializing

Rats should always be in same-sex groups

They are social animals. No matter how much you interact with them, you can never come close to the constant companionship of another rat.

07 — Food

Pellets first, fresh food alongside

  • Oxbow and Mazuri make the best pelleted foods available. Other good brands exist — research them to be sure they're nutritionally balanced and safely manufactured.
  • Rats should also get some fresh fruit and vegetables.
  • Some rats hoard food. If yours do, watch for fresh food going bad or attracting fruit flies.
  • It's usually best not to let adult rats (6+ months) free-feed, to reduce the risk of obesity.

What they can and can't eat

Rats can eat most of the same things humans can — and the same things are bad for them in excess, like sugar and fat — with a few exceptions.

  • A long-repeated “fact” holds that male rats have trouble with fruits containing D-limonene (citrus, mangos). Current information is spotty and conflicting; avoiding the peels and keeping amounts small is likely fine, but be cautious.
  • Certain seeds and pits are toxic to rats.
  • Rat Guide's daily menu figure is an excellent reference.

Three pro tips

01

Unlike dogs and cats, rats can have cooked bones — their jaws aren't strong enough to create dangerous splinters. As long as there's minimal meat left, give leftover bones to your ratties. The bits of meat and marrow are a good source of nutrients, and it encourages healthy chewing. Put them in clean, empty cardboard boxes so the rats really have to work for it.

02

Leftover corn on the cob makes a great treat — just rinse off any excess salt or sauce.

03

Strawberry tops and apple cores, with the seeds removed, are also excellent treats.
