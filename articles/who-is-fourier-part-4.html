Summation, Radians, and a Sneak Peek at Fourier Coefficients

By Darshan · April 27, 2026

Last time we ended with the final parts of the Fourier series formula. To wrap it up, we're going to introduce two tiny tools and a sneak peek into our next Fourier item: the Fourier coefficient.

Now, our first little tool is something called summation. You might be wondering, "Wait, I know sum, but what's summation?" Summation is a symbol representing addition. It's called sigma in the Greek alphabet and looks like a Z that got mutated.

For example, A = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 7. To use summation for this equation, you would just put 7 on the top, the symbol in the middle, n = 1 on the bottom, and n to the right — to say "substitute the numbers 1 through 7 for n one after another and add them together."

Now that you've learned the trick they should have taught us in 1st grade, we can move on to the second tool.

Radians.

There is one more way we haven't talked about to write a Fourier series formula. It's just a different way of writing omega. Unlike the usual ω = 360° × f (or ω = 360°/T), this one uses a new unit: a radian.

One radian is about 57.2958°, and about 6-plus-some-change radians fit into a full circle (360°).

The problem is, we don't know what "some change" is. For all we know it could be 1 million. Luckily for you, it isn't.

We find the exact number by dividing the circumference by r (the radius). How do you do that? Simple.

Circumference = diameter × 3.14

The 3.14 is actually 3.1415... and so on. We call that number pi (π).

Now we plug pi in. Diameter is 2r, so circumference = 2r × π = 2πr.

The number of radians in a full circle is circumference / r, which is 2πr / r = 2π.

A full 360° circle = 2π radians.

And going back to "some change" — 2π ≈ 6.28, which means "some change" is actually just 0.28 radians.

Now we know that a bunch of simple waves add up to a complicated wave. But how do you take a complicated wave and break it down into simple waves? That's where Fourier coefficients come in. They primarily use area.

Three tools picked up: summation, radians, and a peek at Fourier coefficients.
